# Evidence behind each check

Read this before giving evidence levels. Citations were checked against
Crossref in November 2026. If a claim isn't here, don't make it.

## Evidence levels

| Level | Means |
| --- | --- |
| **Field** | A field experiment measured real applications |
| **Legal** | UK Equality Act risk, per ACAS or EHRC guidance. Independent of effect size |
| **Contested** | Good studies disagree |
| **Lab** | Experiments on appeal, belonging or intentions, not real applications |
| **Guidance** | Government or practitioner advice, no trial |
| **Untested** | Nobody has tested it. Say so |

## By check, strongest first

### Pay: a range, and whether it's negotiable (Field)

- Leibbrandt and List (2015): in a field experiment with about 2,500 US job
  seekers, men negotiated more when ads didn't mention negotiation. Saying
  pay was negotiable removed the gap. Men preferred ambiguous pay rules.
  So: unclear terms favour whoever feels entitled to ask.
- Belot, Kircher and Muller (2022): higher posted pay drew more interest.
  This is about pay level, not whether pay is shown.
- The Behavioural Insights Team's 2025 guide rates "state the salary range
  and whether it's negotiable" as effective.
- UK law: a government consultation on requiring pay information in job ads
  closed on 27 October 2026. It is not law yet.

### Flexibility: stated, specific, near the top (Field)

- He, Neumark and Weng (2021): in a field experiment on a Chinese job board,
  jobs advertised as flexible drew more applications at the same salary.
- Government Equalities Office and the Behavioural Insights Team, with
  Indeed (2019 trial, final report 2021): ads offering flexibility drew about
  30% more applicants in round one. The authors say that's likely an
  overestimate. A weaker second round found about 19%. Say "roughly 20 to
  30%, likely an overestimate", or don't quote a number.
- Zurich UK with the Behavioural Insights Team (2019 to 2020, before and
  after, not randomised): advertising all roles as flexible and part-time
  raised women's share of applicants for senior roles from 31% to 37%.
  The report says the cause isn't proven.

### Age wording (Field and Legal)

- Burn, Firoozi, Ladd and Neumark (2024, *Journal of Labor Economics*):
  subtly age-stereotyped wording in real ads across 14 US cities cut the
  share of applicants over 40 by 11.7 percentage points. This is the
  clearest field evidence that ad wording changes who applies.
- ACAS and EHRC give "recent graduate" and "young" as examples of wording
  that can be unlawful unless justified. Long minimum-experience
  requirements can be indirect age discrimination.
- "Digital native" and "energetic" are widely flagged but have no UK
  authority behind them. Call them "commonly flagged".

### Disability: physical proxies and adjustments (Lab and Legal)

- Pike, Powell, Spence and Bourdage (2026, *Journal of Applied
  Psychology*): physical-disability-biased wording in ads lowered perceived
  fit and intention to apply. Equal opportunity and adjustment statements
  raised intention. Two experiments plus an analysis of 1,886 real ads.
- EHRC gives "active person" as an example of risky wording. Disability
  Confident guidance: list essential requirements only, use plain language,
  offer adjustments early.
- No study has tested adjustments wording on real applications. Say so.

### Requirement lists (Field for volume, Lab for gender)

- The "women apply only if they meet 100% of requirements, men at 60%"
  claim traces to an unpublished Hewlett-Packard report. Don't repeat it.
- Behavioural Insights Team (2022, n = 10,468, not peer reviewed): men were
  willing to apply at 52.1% of requirements met, women at 55.7%. The gap
  appeared only among less-qualified people.
- Abraham, Hallermeier and Stein (2024): cutting optional requirements from
  real Uber ads raised applications by about 7%, but didn't raise the share
  of women. Trim requirements for clarity and volume, not as a gender fix.
- "Apply even if you don't meet every requirement" is untested.

### Gendered terms (Legal for titles, Lab for pronouns)

- ACAS gives gendered job titles as examples of risky wording.
- Stout and Dasgupta (2011): generic "he" lowered women's sense of
  belonging in lab studies.
- "Guys" has no source. Suggest a neutral word without citing anything.

### Agentic trait words (Contested)

- Gaucher, Friesen and Kay (2011): five studies. Ads for male-dominated
  jobs used more agentic words. In three small student experiments (43, 96
  and 118 people), agentic wording made jobs less appealing to women, and
  anticipated belonging explained it, not perceived ability.
- Hentschel, Braun, Peus and Frey (2021): agentic wording lowered women's
  anticipated belonging and intention to apply, when the recruiter was a man.
- He and Kang (2025, *PNAS*, 37,920 people, field and lab): replacing
  agentic words with neutral synonyms raised applications from women and
  from men who identify less with masculinity.
- Castilla and Rho (2023, *Management Science*, about 300,000 real
  postings plus a field experiment): effects on who applied were negligible.
- Seong and Parker (2023): Gaucher's effect replicated for start-up ads, not
  for established firms.
- The Behavioural Insights Team's 2025 guide rates reviewing gendered
  wording as promising, and says "no known evidence shows a direct effect on
  application rates".
- So: "may signal you won't fit in; field studies disagree on how much it
  changes who applies; cheap to fix."

### Traits versus behaviours (Lab and Guidance)

- Born and Taris (2010): describing the candidate as behaviours (verbs)
  rather than traits (adjectives) narrowed gender gaps in students'
  inclination to apply.

### Trans and non-binary readers (Lab)

- Hollenbach and Rohmann (2026, German ads): gender-inclusive wording read
  as a sign of safety to trans and non-binary job seekers, and raised their
  willingness to apply. Cis readers were unaffected. One new study.
- No legal advice on gender identity. UK law here is changing.

### Diversity statements (Contested)

- Flory, Leibbrandt, Rott and Stoddard (2021, field experiment): explicit
  signals that a firm valued diversity more than doubled racial minorities'
  interest and applications. Effects by gender weren't significant.
- Kang, DeCelles, Tilcsik and Jun (2016): pro-diversity statements didn't
  reduce bias in an audit study.
- Nichols, Axt, Gosnell and Ariely (2023, field): diverse imagery didn't
  consistently raise applications from women or minorities.
- So: say what you do, specifically. Boilerplate is weak.

### Culture, perks and "rockstar" language (Guidance)

- Cheryan et al. (2009) and Murphy, Steele and Gross (2007) showed that
  stereotyped settings lowered women's belonging. Those were rooms and
  videos, not ads. Commonly advised for ads; untested there.

### Caring stated as practice (Untested as wording)

- No study has tested a line like "Need to duck out for school pick-ups? We
  do too." The practice it points to, flexibility, has the best evidence
  here. Praise it as a signal; don't claim a measured effect.

### Plain language (Guidance)

- Disability Confident guidance asks for plain language. No trial on who
  applies.

like this can support that action. It is independent of B Lab and doesn't
