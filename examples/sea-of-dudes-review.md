# Review: Senior Backend Engineer ("sea of dudes" example)

How it reads

The ad is short and clear about the technology, but its tone may tell many capable engineers that this isn't a place for them.

What's working

- The title is clear and the technical skills are listed plainly. Little else is doing welcoming work yet, so there's room to add.

Three things worth changing

1. Add a pay range, and say whether it's negotiable. Why: "Competitive salary" with no figure counts as missing, and unclear terms favour whoever feels entitled to ask. Evidence: field (Leibbrandt and List, 2015, on negotiability; the Behavioural Insights Team's 2025 guide rates stating the range as effective).
2. Say what flexibility the job offers, near the top. The ad mentions none. Why: in a UK trial, ads offering flexibility drew roughly 20 to 30% more applicants, which the authors say is likely an overestimate. Evidence: field.
3. Soften the "you'll fit in if you're like this" wording: "rockstar", "elite team", "defending your opinions", "dominating the market", the beer fridge and "work hard, play hard". Why: it may signal "you won't fit in" to people who don't see themselves in that picture. Evidence: contested for the agentic trait words (field studies disagree on how much this changes who applies, and it's cheap to fix); guidance only for "rockstar" and the perks. Keep "leading teams" if the job really is leading a team.

Also noticed

- "8+ years": if this job is in the UK, long minimum-experience requirements can be indirect age discrimination (legal risk; ACAS and EHRC guidance). Say what the years were meant to prove.
- "High-energy" is commonly flagged as age-coded, but no UK authority says so. Evidence: untested, my view.
- Seven must-haves, including three "expert-level" languages and four tools. Trimming optional requirements raised applications by about 7% in one field study of real ads, without raising the share of women. Evidence: field, for volume only.
- "A computer science degree from a top university" may stand in for the skills it's meant to prove. Evidence: untested, my view.
- No offer of adjustments at interview or at work. Evidence: lab (Pike and colleagues, 2026), and nobody has tested the wording on real applications.

Who might stop reading

- Someone doing the school run or looking after a parent: stops at the perks list, which has a beer fridge and no flexibility. Would keep reading if the ad said "Core hours are 10 to 3."
- Someone over 50: stops at "high-energy environment". Would keep reading if the ad described the actual work and pace.
- Someone who'd need an adjustment at interview: stops at "Expert-level" with nothing offered. Would keep reading if there was an adjustments line.
- Someone who has never negotiated pay: stops at "Competitive salary". Would keep reading if it gave a range and said it was negotiable.
- Someone without a degree: stops at "from a top university". Would keep reading if it named the skill the degree stood for.

This is a reading exercise, not research.

Word check

Script-adjusted: 0 communal, 14 agentic (raw 15; "competitive salary" tagged as a fixed term). After my read: 0 communal, about 11 agentic worth changing. Discounted: "analytical" and "logical" (the job plausibly needs them), "leading" (if the role leads teams; the ad doesn't say). "Rockstar", "elite" and "high-energy" aren't on the script's lists, so I read for them.

Suggested rewrites

- "Competitive salary" → "[£X to £Y], depending on experience. Pay is negotiable within the range."
- (no flexibility line) → near the top: "Core hours are [10 to 3]. Fit the rest around your life."
- "a driven rockstar engineer to join our elite team" → "an engineer who enjoys [what the work is], in a team that [what the team does]"
- "lead the charge on our core platform, making decisive calls and defending your opinions" → "shape and look after our core platform, make your case, and change your mind when the evidence does"
- "8+ years building distributed systems" → "Experience building [the thing] at [the scale]"
