# Review: Office Coordinator ("balanced but not fine" example)

How it reads

The ad sounds warm, and its word balance looks healthy, but several lines may tell people they aren't wanted.

What's working

- The warm, team-focused opening is a real strength. Keep the communal words as they are.
- It's short, and the job title is clear.

Three things worth changing

1. Remove the age wording: "young, energetic recent graduate" and "digital native". Why: some readers over a certain age will read it as "not for me". Evidence: legal (ACAS and EHRC give "recent graduate" and "young" as examples of wording that can be unlawful unless justified, if this job is in the UK); field (age-stereotyped wording in real ads cut the share of applicants over 40 by 11.7 percentage points, Burn and colleagues, 2024). "Digital native" is commonly flagged, with no UK authority behind it.
2. Tie the physical wording to real tasks. "Fit and active" and a full driving licence for an office job may put off disabled people who could do the work. Why: they describe the person, not a task. Evidence: lab (Pike and colleagues, 2026) and legal (the EHRC gives "active person" as an example of risky wording). Also add an adjustments line.
3. Add a pay range and say whether it's negotiable. "Competitive, depending on experience" counts as no figure. Why: unclear terms favour whoever feels entitled to ask. Evidence: field.

Also noticed

- "He or she will report to our chairman": the title is gendered and "he or she" leaves out people who are neither. Evidence: legal for titles (ACAS), lab for pronouns. Suggest "You'll report to our chair."
- No flexibility mentioned at all. Evidence: field.
- "Committed to understanding each other" is a promise, not proof. Say what it looks like in the week. Evidence: guidance.
- "Confidence and independence" and "a competitive, determined attitude" describe the person, not the work. Evidence: contested.

Who might stop reading

- Someone doing the school run or looking after a parent: stops at the end of the ad, where hours would be. Would keep reading if it said "Core hours are 10 to 3."
- Someone over 50: stops at "young, energetic recent graduate". Would keep reading if it said "early in your career, or returning to work".
- Someone who'd need an adjustment: stops at "fit and active". Would keep reading if the real task was named and adjustments offered.
- Someone who has never negotiated pay: stops at "Competitive, depending on experience". Would keep reading if it gave a range.
- Someone without a degree: "recent graduate" implies one is expected. Would keep reading if the ad said no prior experience is needed.
- Someone trans or non-binary: stops at "He or she" and "chairman". Would keep reading if it said "you" and "chair".

This is a reading exercise, not research.

Word check

Script-adjusted: 5 communal, 4 agentic (raw 7 communal, 5 agentic). After my read: 5 communal, 4 agentic. Discounted: "active" (a physical requirement, handled in item 2, not a trait word), "committed" and "depending" (fixed phrasing). Added by reading: "confidence", which the script didn't catch. The count looks balanced, which is why it hides the problems above. Don't cut the communal words to make it even.

Suggested rewrites

- "a young, energetic recent graduate" → "someone early in your career, or returning to work; no prior experience needed"
- "To be fit and active, as you'll be on your feet a lot" → "Spend about [half] the day on your feet moving around the office" (check the real task)
- "To be a digital native, comfortable with all our systems" → "Comfortable with [the actual tools]"
- "Competitive, depending on experience" → "[£X to £Y], depending on experience. Pay is negotiable within the range."
- "He or she will report to our chairman" → "You'll report to our chair."
